Pig butchering is a long-running investment scam. A stranger builds a friendship or romance with you over weeks, then persuades you to put money into a fake crypto trading platform they control. The "fattening" stage builds trust, and the "butchering" stage is when your deposits, and any extra "fees" to withdraw, are taken.

## Why it is different from a drainer

Most wallet attacks rely on a malicious signature or a fake site, and they are over in seconds. Pig butchering has no exploit. You send the funds yourself, willingly, because you believe the person and the platform are real. That means a hardware wallet, a revoke tool, or a transaction simulator will not stop it. The defense is recognizing the pattern.

The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center has published warnings about these schemes, and news coverage of those warnings describes the same basic structure: contact that starts casually, a growing relationship, and an investment pitch that arrives later.

## How the scam usually unfolds

**1. First contact.** It often looks like a wrong-number text, a friendly message on a social app, a dating profile, or a connection request that turns into chat. The person is warm, attentive, and apparently successful.

**2. Building trust.** Conversation continues for days or weeks. The scammer shares photos, daily routines, and personal stories. Nothing is asked of you yet. The point is to make the relationship feel real and to make you feel it would be rude or paranoid to doubt it.

**3. The investment hint.** The person mentions that they make good money trading crypto, often with a "mentor", a "private analyst", or a "special platform". They offer to show you, not to sell you something.

**4. A small win.** You are guided to a website or app and asked to deposit a small amount. The dashboard shows gains. You may even be allowed to withdraw a little, which is the most persuasive step of all. The platform is fake, and the numbers on screen are just a display.

**5. Bigger deposits.** Encouraged by the apparent profit, you deposit more. Sometimes the scammer suggests borrowing, selling other assets, or using retirement savings.

**6. The lock.** When you try to withdraw a large amount, you are told that you owe taxes, a "verification deposit", or a release fee. Paying never unlocks anything. Eventually the platform stops responding, or the contact disappears.

## Warning signs worth memorizing

- A stranger who contacted you first, and who steers the conversation toward money or crypto within weeks.
- Pressure or excitement about a time-limited opportunity.
- A platform you cannot find independent, reputable coverage of, or one that only accepts crypto deposits to addresses the contact gave you.
- Returns that look unusually steady or large.
- Requests to move conversation to a private messenger.
- Withdrawal blocked behind a new payment.
- A refusal to do a live, unscripted video call, or a call that feels staged.

Any single item can have an innocent explanation. Several together should stop you.

## What to do before sending anything

Pause on principle. A genuine friend will not mind if you take a week. Then check a few things independently:

- Search the platform name together with words like "scam" or "withdrawal", and look at what regulators and established news outlets say rather than at the platform's own testimonials.
- Check whether the platform is a real, licensed exchange by going to the regulator's own site, not a link from the chat.
- Treat the app store as no proof of legitimacy. Fake trading apps have appeared in official stores, and some are installed outside stores through a link.
- Ask whether the person has ever asked you for money directly or indirectly. If the answer involves their platform, that is the same thing.
- Talk to someone you trust offline. Scammers often tell victims to keep it secret, so a second opinion is powerful.

## If you may already be involved

Stop sending money, including "fees" that promise to release your balance. Keep screenshots, wallet addresses, transaction hashes, chat logs, and the platform's address. Report to your national fraud authority; in the United States that includes the FBI's IC3 at ic3.gov, which asks for details like how contact began and which wallets received funds. Recovery is difficult, and be wary of "recovery services" that contact you afterward, since they are a common second scam.

## How Signward helps

Signward is built to flag risky wallet interactions such as suspicious approvals and unfamiliar sites before you sign. It cannot see a conversation with a stranger or decide whether a person is honest, so pig butchering still depends on your own judgment. A warning about a fresh, unknown destination address or site can still be one more moment to pause.

## The short version

If someone you have never met in person is guiding you toward a particular crypto platform, treat that as the red flag, however friendly they are. Real friends do not need you to deposit into their app.
