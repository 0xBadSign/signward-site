A crypto recovery scam is a second fraud aimed at someone who has already lost money. A stranger or a polished website claims they can trace and retrieve your stolen funds, usually for an upfront fee, and then takes that fee and disappears or asks for more. Private firms cannot issue seizure orders, so unsolicited recovery offers should be treated as hostile.

This post explains how the scam is built, the warning signs, and what actually helps after a loss.

## Why victims are targeted twice

Anyone who has just been drained or defrauded is stressed, embarrassed and motivated to act fast. Scammers know this. Victims of fake investment platforms, wallet drainers and phishing often talk about the loss publicly, in forums, on social media or in support chats. Those posts become lead lists.

There is also a structural reason the scam works. Blockchain transfers are final, so real recovery is rare, and most people do not know what is realistic. A confident stranger who says "we do this every day" fills that gap with false hope.

## How the approach usually looks

Recovery scammers reach victims in a few common ways:

- A direct message on social media, Telegram or Discord shortly after you posted about your loss.
- A reply under your public post from an account that "recovered funds for someone like you".
- A search ad or website that claims to be a blockchain forensics firm or a law firm.
- A message claiming to come from law enforcement, an exchange, or a government complaint center.

The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center has published public service announcements describing companies that falsely claim they can recover funds, and fake law firms that contact victims. Both warnings describe the same pattern: unsolicited contact, a confident promise, and a request for money.

## The typical sequence

Most versions follow a similar script:

1. **Contact.** They already seem to know your situation, or ask you to describe it.
2. **Credibility.** They show a professional site, fake case results, fake testimonials or a "tracing report" with real-looking transaction hashes. Public blockchains make it easy to paste in genuine looking data.
3. **The fee.** They ask for an advance payment, often framed as a legal fee, tax, gas cost, or a deposit to "unlock" the recovered funds. Payment is requested in crypto or gift cards.
4. **Escalation.** After you pay, there is always another cost. Funds are "found but frozen", or a court needs one more document.
5. **Data harvesting.** Some also ask for ID, wallet details or a seed phrase "to verify ownership". Anything that asks for a seed phrase is an attempt to steal what is left.

## Warning signs worth memorizing

- They contacted you first. Legitimate investigators and lawyers rarely cold-message crime victims.
- You must pay before any work is done, especially in crypto or gift cards.
- They guarantee a result. Nobody can promise recovery.
- They claim a special partnership with a government agency or an exchange.
- They ask for your seed phrase, private key, remote access to your device, or a wallet connection.
- They pressure you with a deadline.
- A person claiming to be a law enforcement agent contacts you on a chat app. Official agencies publish how they contact people. Verify through an official phone number or office you find yourself, not one they give you.

## What realistically helps after a loss

None of this brings money back by itself, but it limits further damage and keeps real options open.

**Stop the bleeding first.** If a wallet was compromised, move remaining assets to a fresh wallet created on a clean device, with a new seed phrase. Revoke token approvals on the old address where you still can. Do not reuse the old seed.

**Document everything.** Save transaction hashes, wallet addresses, screenshots of chats, websites and payment instructions. Clear records help whoever you report to.

**Report through official channels.** In the United States that includes the FBI's IC3 at ic3.gov, which you should type into the browser yourself rather than reach via a search ad. Other countries have their own cybercrime reporting portals and consumer agencies. Also tell any exchange that received the funds, since they can sometimes freeze deposits when notified quickly.

**Be careful with "tracing" services.** Genuine analytics firms exist and law enforcement does use them, but they work with agencies and large institutions. If you consider hiring any professional, verify them independently, look up their registration yourself, and never pay in crypto upfront to someone who contacted you.

**Talk to someone you trust.** Shame keeps victims quiet, and quiet victims are easier to exploit. A friend or family member can often spot the pressure that you cannot see from inside the situation.

## The short version

If someone you did not contact offers to recover your crypto, assume it is a scam. Do not pay fees up front, never share a seed phrase, secure what you still have, and report through official channels.
