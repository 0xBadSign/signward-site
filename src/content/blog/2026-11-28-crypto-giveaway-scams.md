A crypto giveaway scam is a fake promotion that claims a famous person or project will return more crypto than you send, or reward you for connecting a wallet. Nobody legitimate does this. The "prize" is bait, and the real goal is to take your coins or your approvals.

This post explains the common variants, why the format keeps working, and the quick checks that expose it.

## The two main variants

**Send-and-double.** A post, video or livestream says that if you send a small amount to an address, you will receive double back. The address belongs to the scammer. Once you send, the transaction is final and nothing comes back. There is no hidden step where a reward is released.

**Connect-and-claim.** A page asks you to connect your wallet to "verify eligibility" or "claim a bonus". The page then requests an approval or signature that gives the scammer's contract access to your tokens. This overlaps with the claim scams covered in our post on airdrops, and the same rule applies: a claim that requires an unreadable signature deserves suspicion.

Both variants depend on you acting before you think. That is why they come with countdown timers, "limited slots" and a message that the offer ends today.

## Why they look convincing

The presentation is the main tool. Scammers borrow credibility in several ways:

- **Hijacked or lookalike accounts.** A real account with a large following can be taken over, or a near-identical handle can be created. Replies and quote posts are often flooded with fake accounts that "confirm" they received their reward.
- **Edited or synthetic video.** Old interviews or conference clips are re-uploaded, sometimes with altered audio or a fabricated voice, and a QR code or address is placed on screen. Treat any video of a public figure promising free crypto as fake by default.
- **Fabricated proof.** Screenshots of "payouts" and transaction hashes are easy to forge or to arrange between accounts the scammer controls.
- **Fake urgency from the crowd.** Comments such as "it worked, thanks!" are written by the same operation.

None of this needs advanced technology. It needs a trusted face and a short window of attention.

## Why the offer cannot be real

Each section here can stand on its own, so here is the logic in plain terms. A blockchain transfer cannot be reversed by the sender, and a company has no reason to ask for money first in order to give you more. If a project wanted to reward users, it would distribute tokens to eligible addresses or publish a claim process on its official, verified channels, and it would never require you to pay in advance. Sending funds to receive more funds is the defining shape of the scam, and it is a reliable red flag by itself.

Honest campaigns also tend to be boring. They name a snapshot date, a published eligibility rule and a claim page linked from the project's own site. They do not ask you to hurry.

## Quick checks before you act

1. **Do not send anything to receive anything.** If the offer starts with a payment from you, stop.
2. **Find the announcement on the official site.** Navigate to the project's website yourself, not through the post, and look for the same offer there.
3. **Check the account carefully.** Compare the exact handle, the account age and the follower history. Check whether replies are limited or filtered.
4. **Treat any connect request skeptically.** A real claim never needs a seed phrase, and a signature request should be readable. If the wallet shows a vague prompt, decline.
5. **Ignore the clock.** Pressure is part of the design. Waiting a day costs nothing if the offer is genuine.
6. **Use a separate wallet for anything experimental.** If you do interact with a new site, do it with a wallet that holds little.

## If you already sent funds or approved something

If you sent coins, the transfer cannot be undone, so do not chase it through anyone who offers to "recover" it, since that is usually a second scam. Keep the transaction hash and the account links as evidence, and consider reporting it to your local consumer protection or cybercrime authority and to public scam trackers such as Chainabuse, which helps others check addresses.

If you connected a wallet and signed something, review and revoke token approvals promptly, and move remaining assets to a fresh wallet if you shared a seed phrase.
